CIVILIZATION, REBOOTED: CAMERA OBSCURA

If you were stranded in the past, or rebuilding from scratch, which
inventions could you explain well enough to make? Civilization, Rebooted
explores that question one invention at a time: what it does, how it works,
what materials it needs, and what it makes possible next. Think practical
curiosity, with things to build and try. A complete survival manual would
need rather more pages.

We begin with a camera obscura: a dark box or room with a small hole that
projects a live image of the outside scene onto the opposite surface. The
image moves as the scene moves; nothing is recorded. No lens or electricity
is required. It is a useful first step toward understanding optics and the
image-making that later cameras build upon.

Our version uses a shoebox. Add a pinhole and a paper screen, and a branch
outside appears inside, waving upside down. The shoes were probably more
complicated.

Camera obscura means "dark chamber." Long before photography, people used
room-size and portable versions for drawing and entertainment. Today we'll
build one, watch the picture appear, and work out why it turns the world
over.

THE HOLE DOES THE SORTING

Imagine a tree outside your box. Light reflected from every part of it
spreads in many directions. Without a front wall, light from different parts
would overlap across your screen. You would get illumination, but no
recognizable tree.

The wall blocks almost everything. Its tiny opening admits only a narrow
bundle from each point in the scene. In ordinary air, those rays travel
essentially straight. Light from the treetop travels downward through the
hole and keeps going downward, reaching the bottom of the screen. Light from
the foot of the trunk travels upward and reaches the top.

Draw those two paths and they cross at the hole. Nothing has grabbed the
tree and turned it over. Geometry has done the job quietly.

BUILD A SMALL DARK CHAMBER

Gather a shoebox with its lid, aluminum foil, tracing paper, opaque tape,
scissors, a fine pin, and a dark towel or cloth. Use translucent tracing
paper, not ordinary opaque drawing paper. An adult should handle sharp-tool
work for children; cut on a protected surface, away from hands, and put the
pin away afterward.

1. Choose the two short ends of the box. In one, cut a square opening about
an inch across. Tape flat foil over it, sealing every edge. This is the
front.

2. Prick one small, clean hole in the center of the foil. Start with just
the pin's tip, roughly half a millimeter across if you can manage it. Don't
shove the whole shaft through. Replace torn foil rather than trying to mend
the hole.

3. In the opposite end, cut a window several inches across, leaving a sturdy
cardboard border. Stretch tracing paper flat across it and tape all four
edges. This is your screen. Leave it intact: you will look at the paper from
outside the box.

4. Fit the lid and seal its seams, corners, and any accidental gaps with
opaque tape. Light should enter through the pinhole; the screen is the one
deliberately translucent wall.

5. Set up in shade or a dim room facing a brightly lit outdoor scene. A
building or tree with strong light-and-dark shapes makes a better first
subject than a blank wall. Keep the Sun out of the view. Never aim this
viewer at the Sun or look through its pinhole at the Sun; this project is
not a solar viewer.

6. Point the foil end toward your subject. Look at the tracing-paper end
from a comfortable reading distance. Drape the dark cloth over the screen,
your head, and the space between them to block stray light. Leave the front
opening uncovered and keep breathing space. Stay still while your eyes
adjust.

A dim, inverted picture should emerge. This is a live projection on the
paper. Cover the pinhole with a finger and it vanishes immediately. Uncover
it and the world resumes.

TROUBLESHOOT LIKE AN OPTICIAN

Nothing visible? Shade the viewing side more thoroughly, check that the hole
is open, and try a brighter subject. Ordinary room lighting often
disappoints. Bright scenery and a genuinely dark viewing space are the
useful combination.

Then try a fresh foil patch with a slightly larger hole. More light gets in,
so the image brightens, but each point spreads over a larger patch of
screen. Detail blurs. A smaller hole usually sharpens the picture while
making it dimmer, up to a limit: make it extremely small and light's wave
behavior, called diffraction, spreads the image again. "Smaller" eventually
stops meaning "better."

Keep both foil patches and compare them on the same scene. You have acquired
an aperture control for approximately the cost of a sandwich wrapper.

There is no shutter to press. Watch a branch move, then put your hand over
the opening and restore the darkness. For a moment, a cardboard box has let
you see how a picture happens. That is a respectable afternoon's work for
something rescued from a closet.
